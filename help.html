mutant.kiwi Imagery Explorer

Browse and stream 621,728 New Zealand aerial imagery sheets spanning 1940–2025 — directly in your browser.

Getting Started

1
Choose a basemap — Use the dropdown in the top-left to switch between NZ Topo Lite, NZ Topo (Gridless), and NZ Aerial Imagery.
2
Navigate to a location — Pan and zoom the map, or use the search box to find a place by name.
3
Click the map — Click anywhere to query all 621,728 imagery sheets at that location. Results appear in the panel on the right, grouped by region and sorted newest first.
4
Load a sheet — Click any result card to stream it directly onto the map as a Cloud-Optimised GeoTIFF. No downloads, no waiting.
Some sections below are marked Beta. Those features live only on the beta viewer at imagery.mutant.kiwi/beta.html, which is the same map plus historic survey photographs. Everything else on this page works on both.

Compass Navigator

New. Right-click any loaded sheet → Add more sheets to open the compass rose.

Each button loads the adjacent sheet from the same imagery series in that direction:

  • Blue buttons — a same-series neighbour sheet is available
  • Greyed buttons — no neighbour available, or already loaded

The compass closes automatically after loading. Press Esc or click outside to dismiss.

All compass navigation stays within the same series — resolution, date and sensor are always consistent across adjacent tiles.

Sharing Links

Two buttons sit at the top of the results panel. Both copy a link straight to your clipboard and show a brief confirmation. The links work for anyone — no login needed.

Save Search

Shares where you were looking. The link records the map position and zoom, and re-runs the imagery query when opened, so the recipient sees the same list of sheets.

Save Project

Shares what you built. Everything on the map travels with the link:

  • the basemap you chose, including the historic series
  • every imagery sheet you loaded, each at its own opacity, and hidden ones stay hidden
  • every WMTS layer you added
  • on the beta viewer, the Retrolens photo you have open and the exact corner positions you dragged it to

Open the link and the map rebuilds itself — same place, same background, same layers, same transparency.

ButtonWhat the recipient sees
Save SearchMap flies to the location and the results panel opens
Save ProjectThe whole map as you left it, layers and all
Right-click a sheet → Share image linkMap flies to the location and loads that one sheet
Project links are short — around sixty characters however much you have loaded — so they survive being pasted into chat apps and emails. Occasionally you may get a much longer link instead: that is the fallback, it works exactly the same, it is just more awkward to paste.
If the button says view only — nothing on the map, there was no imagery loaded when you clicked. The link still takes someone to the right place on the right basemap, but it will not carry any sheets.
Opening someone else's project will not disturb your own work. Any photo positions you have adjusted yourself are kept; the link's versions are added alongside them.

WMTS Tiles

Many LINZ surveys are also published as ready-made map tiles. Where one exists, the result card offers it as an alternative to streaming the sheet itself. Tiles draw faster and cover the whole survey rather than a single sheet, so they are usually the better choice when you want context rather than one exact frame.

ButtonWhat it does
Add WMTSPuts the survey's tiles on the map. The button becomes Hide WMTS to take them off again
Copy Tile URLCopies the tile address to your clipboard, ready to paste into any other mapping tool
QGIS layerDownloads a .qlr file — open it in QGIS and the layer appears, correctly placed in NZTM2000
ArcGIS layerDownloads a .lyrx file for ArcGIS Pro

A WMTS layer appears in the sheets on map bar alongside any imagery you have loaded, so it gets the same opacity slider, visibility toggle and remove button.

Not every survey has tiles. Where LINZ has not published a layer for a collection, the WMTS buttons simply do not appear on that card. Near-infrared surveys never have them — LINZ publishes no infrared tile layers.
The map uses Web Mercator tiles, because that is all a web map can read. The QGIS and ArcGIS files point at the NZTM2000 version instead, which is the right one for desktop GIS work.

Historic Photos Beta

The beta viewer can search a second dataset: roughly 345,000 scanned aerial survey photographs from Retrolens, going back to the 1930s — decades earlier than the oldest LINZ imagery.

1
Use the Dataset dropdown at the top left to switch between Aerial Imagery and Retrolens Historic Photos.
2
Click the map as usual. Results now show photo thumbnails — click one for a full-size view.
3
Open a photo on the map to position it over the ground (see below).
Where a survey has since been scanned and properly georeferenced as modern imagery, its loose photos are hidden by default — the georeferenced version is better in every way, and showing both just clutters the list.

Georeferencing a Photo Beta

A Retrolens scan is just a photograph: it has no idea where it belongs on the map. The viewer makes a first guess from the flight record — the camera position, altitude and focal length — which usually lands close. You then drag it into place by hand.

1
Open a photo on the map. A panel appears at the bottom with the photo's name.
2
Drag the corner dots to stretch the image, or the square handle in the middle to move the whole thing. The buttons rotate, enlarge and shrink it in small steps.
3
Drop the Opacity slider to around half so you can see the ground underneath while you line up roads, coastline or river bends.
4
Click Save fit. Your positioning is remembered for that photo and comes back next time you open it.

Getting it out of the browser

ButtonWhat you get
World fileA .jgw and .prj to sit alongside the full-size JPG. Most GIS software reads this pair automatically
QGIS pointsA points file for the QGIS Georeferencer — the exact option, see below
GDALCopies a ready-made command to your clipboard for building a proper GeoTIFF
ResetBack to the original guess from the flight record
World file or QGIS points? A world file can only describe a transform that keeps opposite edges parallel — move, rotate, scale and skew. If you have dragged the corners into a genuine trapezoid, which is normal for an oblique photo, no world file can reproduce that exactly. The viewer fits the closest one it can and tells you how far off it is. If that number matters to you, use QGIS points instead, which carries your corners exactly.
How accurate is this? Good enough to see what was where; not survey grade. Stretching a photo by its four corners cannot undo lens distortion, camera tilt, or the way hills lean towards or away from the camera. Expect a good fit on flat ground and visible drift in steep country. Treat it as a careful overlay, not a measurement.
Saved fits live in your own browser, so clearing site data loses them. They do travel in a Save Project link, which is the way to send a positioned photo to someone else — and opening their link will not disturb any fits of your own.

Tips

  • Zoom in for better image quality — COGs stream progressively so detail improves as you zoom
  • Older sheets (1940s–1980s) are scanned film — expect lower contrast and slight colour cast
  • If a sheet appears blank, zoom in — it may be outside its valid extent at your current zoom level
  • The first click in a new area may be slightly slower as the spatial index is fetched (~300 KB)
